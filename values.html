How I work

I learn how a process works by asking the people who use it. A design has to account for their working conditions and the time and knowledge they can bring to it.

Working principles

I try to remove confusion by making responsibilities and expectations explicit. Honest communication should also be considerate. People need to know what is happening and what is expected of them, particularly when the work is difficult.

I value calm, accountable leadership and the courage to address a problem. Perfect information is rare; I want people to understand what is known and who owns the next step so the work can continue.

I have a responsibility to use my skills for the team’s benefit. That includes the unglamorous work: backlogs, documentation, cleanup, first drafts, and follow-up. Getting close to that work often reveals why a process is failing.

AI & technology in education

My work in technical support taught me to start with what a person needs to do. In instructional design, I choose technology for the learning it enables and whether people can use it.

Access and usability. Captions, translation, reading support, and image descriptions can remove barriers to participation. AI-generated versions need to be checked for accuracy and tested with the people using them.

Information and judgment. AI can help with brainstorming, explanations, and practice. Learners also need to question answers and verify sources, including recognizing when AI would bypass the thinking they need to develop.

Connection with people. Technology can bring teachers, mentors, peers, and resources within reach. AI should support those relationships.

Support for the educator. AI can help draft examples, adapt reading levels, build practice scenarios, and summarize feedback. The educator remains responsible for accuracy, ethics, developmental appropriateness, and the quality of the learning experience.

Teaching responsible use includes teaching when to refrain from using AI. I want these tools to expand access and give educators more time for work that needs their judgment and care.

In practice

I use observation, questions, and process review to understand where people are getting stuck. When I write a project plan, status update, or job aid, I consider what the reader needs to decide or do. In learning design, I plan for application and practice.

The Personalized Learning Journey pilot describes a decision to involve managers because of their knowledge of the advisor and their established relationship. My AI workflow examples describe how I organize project context and reusable resources.

After the handoff

I want someone else to be able to maintain the work and adapt it when conditions change. That requires documentation and an understanding of the method. I also want to know whether they can use the approach and achieve the intended result.

In the Discovery Series, learners created work they could hand over, and managers reviewed it against the course requirements.
